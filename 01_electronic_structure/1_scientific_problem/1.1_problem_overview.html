Pillar 1: Complex PDEs & Eigenvalue Problems

1.1 Electronic Structure: Problem Overview

Translating the quantum many-body problem into high-dimensional PDEs, 3D grids, and nonlinear eigenvalue solvers for computer architects and compiler engineers.

Why Electrons Govern Materials & Chemistry

Every macroscopic property of a molecule or crystal—whether a battery cathode conducts lithium ions, a catalyst lowers an activation barrier, or a semiconductor has a 1.1 eV bandgap—is determined by how its electrons arrange themselves around atomic nuclei.

Physical Intuition: Nuclei are at least $1836\times$ heavier than electrons (proton/electron mass ratio). On the timescale of nuclear motion, electrons relax almost instantaneously to their lowest-energy quantum state (the ground state).
Compiler / Architect Translation: Electronic structure is an inner-loop eigenvalue PDE solver. Before you can move atoms by a single femtosecond in a simulation, you must solve a coupled 3D partial differential equation (or generalized matrix eigenvalue problem) to obtain the total energy $E$ and atomic forces $\mathbf{F}_I = -\nabla_{\mathbf{R}_I} E$.

The Core Equations: From Exponential Curse to 3D Grids

1. Born-Oppenheimer Approximation (Decoupling Nuclei & Electrons)

For $M$ nuclei at fixed positions $\{\mathbf{R}_I\}$ and $N$ electrons at coordinates $\{\mathbf{r}_i\}$, the Born-Oppenheimer approximation freezes the nuclei as a static external potential $V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r})$ and solves the time-independent electronic Schrödinger equation:

$$\hat{H}_{\text{elec}}\,\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N) = E\,\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N)$$ $$\hat{H}_{\text{elec}} = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^N \nabla_i^2 + \sum_{i=1}^N V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_i) + \sum_{i < j}^N \frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|}$$

The Curse of Dimensionality: $\Psi$ lives in $3N$-dimensional space (plus spin) and must be antisymmetric under particle exchange: $\Psi(\dots,\mathbf{r}_i,\dots,\mathbf{r}_j,\dots) = -\Psi(\dots,\mathbf{r}_j,\dots,\mathbf{r}_i,\dots)$. Discretizing $\Psi$ on a grid of just 10 points per axis for $N=100$ electrons requires $10^{300}$ floats—far exceeding atoms in the universe.

2. Kohn-Sham Density Functional Theory (KS-DFT)

Instead of tracking a $3N$-dimensional wavefunction $\Psi$, Hohenberg-Kohn theorems prove that ground-state energy is uniquely determined by the 3D electron density $\rho(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^N |\psi_i(\mathbf{r})|^2$. Kohn-Sham DFT maps the interacting many-body problem onto $N$ non-interacting single-electron orbitals $\psi_i(\mathbf{r})$ moving in an effective potential $V_{\text{eff}}[\rho](\mathbf{r})$:

$$\left[ -\frac{1}{2}\nabla^2 + V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}) + V_{\text{Hartree}}[\rho](\mathbf{r}) + V_{\text{XC}}[\rho](\mathbf{r}) \right] \psi_i(\mathbf{r}) = \varepsilon_i \psi_i(\mathbf{r})$$

Because $V_{\text{Hartree}}$ and the unknown exchange-correlation potential $V_{\text{XC}}$ depend on $\rho(\mathbf{r})$, which depends on $\{\psi_i\}$, this is a nonlinear, self-consistent eigenvalue problem.

Intuitive Examples & Real-World Impact

1. H₂ Bond Breaking
At equilibrium ($0.74\,\text{\AA}$), two electrons share a bonding orbital. Stretching H₂ breaks the spin-paired bond into two open-shell atoms—a classic strong correlation failure mode for standard DFT functionals, requiring multi-reference wavefunctions or neural VMC.
2. Silicon Crystal & Defects
A primitive Si cell has 2 atoms, but modeling a single dopant, dislocation, or oxide interface requires a supercell of 500–10,000+ atoms. Because DFT scales as $O(N^3)$, a $10\times$ larger cell costs $1000\times$ more compute.
3. Heterogeneous Catalysis
Predicting ammonia synthesis (N₂ dissociation on Fe/Ru surfaces) requires chemical accuracy ($\le 1\,\text{kcal/mol} \approx 43\,\text{meV}$). A $0.1\,\text{eV}$ error in barrier height changes predicted reaction rates by $\sim 50\times$ at room temperature.
